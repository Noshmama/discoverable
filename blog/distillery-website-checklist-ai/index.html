The Distillery Website Checklist for Getting Found by AI

More buyers are asking an assistant — “a high-rye bourbon for an Old Fashioned,” “a smooth sipping tequila under $80” — and the AI names a handful. Here’s how to make sure yours is on that list.

Whether your distillery gets named has surprisingly little to do with how good the liquid is. An assistant builds its answer from what it can read about you — your site, your listings, the review apps, the enthusiast forums, the press. So the distilleries that get recommended are the ones easiest for a machine to understand. Here’s the checklist I’d run on any spirits site, with the jargon defined as we go. (There’s a winery version too — the moves rhyme, the specifics differ.)

1. Get your words out of pictures and PDFs

Tasting notes, mash bills, awards, the story of the still — distilleries love to post these as beautiful images or downloadable spec sheets. A machine can barely read either. Quick test: try to highlight your bourbon’s tasting note with your cursor. If you can’t, neither can the AI. Put the words on the page as real text. (Here’s how to write a spirits tasting note a machine can use.)

2. Answer the questions buyers actually ask — in writing

The category and proof, the age, the mash bill, where it’s made, cocktail suggestions, whether you offer tours or tastings, and where to buy. One that matters more in spirits than most categories: do you distill it yourself or source and bottle it? Buyers and the enthusiast communities AI leans on ask this constantly — being plainly factual about it builds trust a machine can act on.

3. Go by one name, everywhere

Your distillery name, your brand and expression names, your proof and age statements — they need to match across your own site, Distiller, Whiskybase, Total Wine, Google, and your distributors’ listings. When your brand shows up three slightly different ways, you teach the machine you’re three faint strangers instead of one trusted name.

4. Add the “hidden labels” (structured data)

There’s a small chunk of code you can add to a page that never shows on screen — its only job is to tell a machine what each thing is: “this is a distillery,” “this is a product,” “this is a 4.3-star review.” It’s called structured data (the technical name is schema.org markup), and it’s like the tag sewn into a shirt collar — the visitor never sees it, but it spells everything out. Most distillery sites skip it, which leaves the machine guessing.

How to actually add it — you won’t hand-write it:

  1. Decide what to mark up. The usual three: your distillery as a business (name, location, hours), each expression as a product, and your ratings and reviews.
  2. Have it generated. If your site runs on Shopify, Squarespace, or WordPress, a built-in setting or an SEO/schema plugin (like Yoast) can add it for you. If it’s a custom site, your web developer drops in a short snippet — a format called JSON-LD, which is just structured data written as a small block of text in the page’s code.
  3. Confirm it worked. Paste the page’s web address into Google’s free Rich Results Test — it reads the page and tells you exactly what structured data it found.

The one-line instruction for whoever runs your site: “add schema.org structured data for the distillery, the products, and reviews, and verify it in Google’s Rich Results Test.”

5. Make sure you’re not locked out

Search engines and AI send out automated readers (called crawlers) to read your pages. A full-screen “Are you 21?” wall — or a heavy drag-and-drop site build — can accidentally block those readers, so they see nothing. You usually don’t need to drop the age check; you need it built so the page still loads underneath for the crawlers. If they can’t get in, you can never be recommended, so it’s worth having someone check.

6. Earn the mentions AI already trusts

Coverage in the spirits press, a real presence on the review apps and enthusiast communities (Distiller, Whiskybase, the bourbon and whiskey subreddits), medals from competitions buyers recognize, and genuine reviews. These are the sources an assistant leans on to decide you’re real and worth naming. It’s the slowest of the six — and the most durable.

The test

Open ChatGPT and type “recommend a distillery or a bottle like [a peer of yours].” Are you named? Try Google’s AI answer and Perplexity too. If you’re not there, one of the six above is why — and every one is fixable.

One honest note on timing

None of this is instant. After you make the changes, search engines have to re-read your pages and rebuild their picture of you, which takes weeks. It’s a build, not an afternoon’s work — but it compounds, and it costs no ad budget.
