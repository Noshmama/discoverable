How to Get Your Spirits Named When Someone Asks AI for a Holiday Gift

Every December, more people skip the liquor store and ask an assistant for a bottle to give. Here’s how to be the one it names, and the shipping catch that trips up distilleries but not wineries.

Short answer: When someone asks ChatGPT or Gemini for “a good whiskey gift under $100,” it names a few distilleries and skips the rest. Getting named isn’t about awards. It’s about whether your site answers a gift-buyer’s real question, occasion, budget, recipient, and states the decisive facts in plain text, especially where you can legally ship, which for spirits is far more limited than wine. Build that page now, in the slow weeks, not in December.

More buyers are skipping the aisle and just asking: “a bottle of bourbon for my brother-in-law, under $100, that arrives before Christmas.” The assistant names a few options and moves on. If yours isn’t one of them, it usually isn’t quality. It’s that your website doesn’t answer the gift question, and for spirits there’s an extra trap most sites walk right into: shipping.

A page that answers gift questions, not just a product grid

Gift-buyers ask by occasion, budget, and recipient, never by mash bill. A page organized around “gifts under $50,” “gifts under $100,” “for a bourbon lover,” “for someone new to whiskey” gives the machine something to match. A wall of product tiles gives it nothing.

The shipping reality spirits can’t hide

This is the part wineries don’t have to worry about the way you do. Direct-to-consumer spirits shipping is far more restricted than wine, only a handful of states allow it, and many don’t at all. A gift-buyer’s very first question is “will it actually reach them.” So your page has to say plainly which states you can ship to, and if you can’t ship to the recipient, where they can buy it instead: the retailers that carry you, or local pickup. An assistant won’t recommend a gift it can’t confirm is deliverable, and a buyer won’t forgive a bottle that can’t arrive. Being upfront about shipping isn’t a weakness here. It’s the tactic.

The decisive facts, in plain text

Category and style (bourbon, rye, single malt, blanco tequila, London dry gin), proof, age statement if there is one, price, the order-by date, whether there’s gift packaging or a gift message, and the shipping states. Put all of it as real text on the page, not buried in a PDF or baked into an image where a machine can’t read it. (More on that in the distillery website checklist.)

Occasion and budget language, not just tasting notes

“Notes of clove and toasted oak” reads beautifully and tells the machine nothing a gift-buyer asked. Add the plain layer underneath: “a smooth bourbon for someone new to whiskey, under $60,” or “a peaty, Islay-style single malt for a serious drinker.” That’s the language people actually type. (Here’s how to write a spirits tasting note an AI will quote.)

Enough outside signal to be trusted

AI leans on sources it already knows: the retailers and review sites in your category, press, genuine reviews. If credible places carry and mention you, the machine is willing to put your name in an answer. If nobody does, it has no reason to.

Why now, and not December

Search engines and AI need weeks to find and index a new page. If you build your gift page in December, you’ve missed the window. If you build it now, in the slow autumn weeks, it’s live, indexed, and tested before the searches start. (The same timing logic applies to wine, in the winery version of this post.)

The distillery that gets recommended this season isn’t the most awarded one. It’s the one that answered the real question, honest shipping answer included, first.
