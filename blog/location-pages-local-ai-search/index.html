Location Pages: How Local Businesses (and Wineries) Get Found One Town at a Time

The most underused move in local and AI search — done right. The seven parts of a real location page, the trap that gets you penalized, and how it applies to a winery.

Short answer: A location page is a dedicated page for a specific place you serve. Done right, it wins narrow, high-intent local searches (“[what you do] in [town]”) and gives search engines and AI one clear, matchable answer for that place. Done wrong — cookie-cutter pages with the town name swapped in — it gets you penalized as spam. The whole game is building a genuinely different page only where you have something real to say.

When I built WellTeenMD.com, a pediatric practice in Los Angeles, one move did more for its visibility than anything else: a separate page for each community it serves. Not one “Areas We Serve” page with a list of towns. A real page for Encino, another for Tarzana, another for Sherman Oaks, Studio City, Van Nuys, and their neighbors, each with its own directions, its own local detail, its own reason to exist. Within a week the practice was on page one for local searches, and the AI answer engines stopped garbling where it operated. That’s the power of a location page, and it’s one of the most underused moves in local search. (It’s also how you fix an AI that’s describing your locations wrong, and it was a lead player in the page-one-in-a-week case study.)

Here’s why it works. A local business gets found one town at a time. Someone doesn’t search “adolescent medicine” in the abstract; they search “GLP-1 doctor for teens in the San Fernando Valley,” or just “pediatrician in Tarzana.” A page built around that exact place is a page that can win that exact search, because almost no competitor bothered to build one. And it does double duty: it hands a machine an unambiguous fact — this business serves this town — instead of leaving it to guess. When you don’t give the machine that fact plainly, it fills the gap with a guess, and the guess is often wrong.

But there’s a rule that separates a location page from a penalty, and it’s the part people get wrong. The pages have to be genuinely different. Real directions, real local detail, real reasons someone in that town would care. What Google punishes — it calls them doorway pages — is the cookie-cutter version: fifty identical pages with nothing changed but the place name. That’s not serving a town; it’s spamming a keyword, and the search engines are very good at spotting it. For WellTeenMD I even cut a few towns that were a stretch, because a thin page for a place you barely serve does more harm than good. Winnable searches, one honest page each. That’s the whole formula.

What actually goes on a location page

A real one has seven parts. If you can fill all seven honestly, build the page. If you’d have to pad it, that’s your sign the place doesn’t qualify.

  1. The place in the title, headline, and URL. “Teen GLP-1 Weight Care in Tarzana,” not “Our Services.” That’s what a machine matches to a “[thing] in [town]” search.
  2. The local answer in the first sentence. Who you are, what you do, and that you serve this specific place — before any story.
  3. Detail that’s only true of that place. Directions from a local landmark, parking, which neighborhoods you draw from, travel time, what’s nearby. This one section is the entire line between a real page and a doorway page.
  4. Your offer, framed for that town. Hours, what someone here gets, how to start.
  5. Local proof, if you have it. A review or note from a customer in that area does more than a generic testimonial.
  6. The facts a machine can read. Your name, address, and service area as plain text and in structured data. (This is the one to hand your developer: “add LocalBusiness schema with the address and area served to each location page.”) Same name, address, and phone everywhere.
  7. Links in and out, and a clear next step. A main “locations” page linking to each town page, each town page linking back to your core pages, and one obvious action — book, visit, call, buy.

How this applies to a winery

Carefully, and honestly — because a winery is not a service business covering thirty neighborhoods, and pretending otherwise is exactly the doorway-page trap. You can’t spin up “wine in Cleveland,” “wine in Austin,” “wine in Miami” pages for towns you have no real connection to. That backfires.

But there are three legitimate versions, and most wineries have built none of them:

Your tasting room and the towns around it. If people visit you, you have real local intent to capture: “wine tasting near [your town],” “wineries in [your AVA],” “[nearby town] wine tasting with a view.” A genuine page about visiting you, with directions, what the experience is like, and what’s nearby, wins the visitor who’s planning a trip. That’s a real place you actually occupy.

Where to buy, by market. If your distribution or shipping varies by state, “where to buy [your winery] in [state]” or “wineries that ship to [state]” pages answer a real, high-intent question — and they tie directly to the facts a machine needs to recommend you as a gift or a purchase. The key word is real: build the page only for states where the answer is genuinely yes.

Genuine visitor and region content. If you’re in wine country, the searches around visiting your region — where to go, what to pair, what’s worth the drive — are honest local intent you can serve better than a generic travel site, because you’re actually there.

What a winery page actually looks like. Say you’re in Healdsburg. A real “Wine Tasting in Healdsburg” page opens by telling a visitor where you are and what the tasting is, then gives directions from the town plaza, where to park, the best time to come, and what else is worth doing nearby — then your tasting options, hours, and a reservation button, with your address marked up for machines. Compare that to a “Where to Buy Our Wine in Texas” page, which is a completely different animal: which retailers carry you, whether you ship there, and how to order. Same tactic, two genuinely different pages, each answering a real question for a real place. Neither could be produced by swapping a town name into a template — which is exactly the point.

The test for every one of these is the same test WellTeenMD passed: could you write a full, useful, different page about this place that a person would be glad to land on? If yes, build it. If you’d have to pad it with the town name and boilerplate, don’t. One honest page for a place you truly serve beats ten thin ones for places you don’t, every time.
