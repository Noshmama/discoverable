Page One in a Week

A case study in local and AI search.

Short answer: A brand-new local website can reach Google’s first page, and get quoted by name in AI answers, within about a week, if you target specific, low-competition local searches instead of broad head terms. The playbook: aim at winnable searches, add structured data, open pages with plain answers, prove real expertise with “same as” links, build a genuine page per city, claim your Google Business Profile, and use Search Console to request indexing. Here’s the case study, with honest fine print.

This spring we built WellTeenMD.com from an empty folder: a new marketing site for Dr. Ron Bahar, a Los Angeles pediatric gastroenterologist with more than 30 years of experience, launching GLP-1 weight care for teens. Within a week of launch it was on page one of Google for real searches, and being quoted by name inside Google’s AI answer. Here’s exactly what we did, and the honest fine print underneath the headline.

The result, honestly

  • Page one for “GLP-1 doctors for teens in the San Fernando Valley”: listed twice on the same page, above a nationally known pediatric-obesity specialist.
  • Cited by name in Google’s AI answer for the category: the practice quoted directly, not just linked.
  • About seven days from launch to those rankings: for specific, high-intent local searches, not broad head terms. That distinction is the whole strategy.

The move most people get wrong

Most new sites chase the biggest, most-searched phrases, the short, obvious ones like “weight loss,” “family doctor,” or “personal injury lawyer.” Those are exactly the phrases already owned by billion-dollar companies and decades-old institutions, and a two-week-old site has no chance against them.

The trick to ranking fast is the opposite instinct: go after the searches you can actually win, specific, local, and exactly what your ideal customer would type. You trade a huge audience for a real position on the page. And for a local business, a handful of the exact-right searchers beats a flood of the wrong ones. Everything below is a variation on that one idea.

Play 1: Aim at searches you can win

We didn’t target “weight-loss doctor.” We targeted “GLP-1 doctors for teens in the San Fernando Valley”: three narrowing details at once (the treatment + the age group + the neighborhood) that almost no one else had built a page around. Hardly anyone searches that exact phrase, so there was almost no competition, and every single person who does search it is precisely the patient the practice wants. That page reached the first page of results within a week.

Play 2: Help Google (and AI) understand what you are

Search engines and AI tools don’t read your website the way a person does. They rely on something called structured data.

Structured data, also called schema markup, is a small, standardized block of code, added to a page, that lists the key facts about your business in a format machines can read directly: your name, address, and hours; each doctor and their credentials; your services; your FAQs. Think of it as a fact sheet written in a language every search engine and AI tool already understands. It sits invisibly in the page’s code (visitors never see it), but it’s what lets Google and the AI answer tools understand your site with confidence instead of guessing.

This is the one spot where you hand your web developer a specific instruction. Tell them: “Add structured data (schema markup) to every page, for the business, the people, the services, and the FAQs.” It’s a standard, well-documented practice, so any competent developer will know exactly what you mean. It’s one of the biggest reasons a machine will trust your site, and quote it.

Play 3: Write answers, not just marketing copy

The AI answer tools (Google’s AI Overview, ChatGPT, Perplexity) quote pages that state things clearly and simply. So we opened each important page with a short, direct answer to the exact question a parent would ask (“Are GLP-1 medications safe for teens?”), before any salesmanship. Within days, the practice was being cited by name in Google’s AI answer for its category. That’s the new front page: not a link someone clicks, but a sentence the AI reads out loud with your name attached.

Play 4: Prove you’re the real thing

For health topics especially, Google rewards genuine expertise and trust. The practice has two real, board-certified physicians, so we said so plainly, and then we connected the new website to the doctors’ existing, well-known profiles: their pages on the hospital systems and doctor directories where Google has trusted them for years.

In the code, those connections are called “same as” links: you’re literally telling Google, the doctor on this brand-new site is the same person as the doctor on that trusted page you’ve known for a decade. (Another one to hand your developer: “Add ‘same as’ links from our site to our established profiles.”) That borrowed credibility is something a new website simply can’t build on its own overnight.

Play 5: Build a page for each place you serve (City Pages)

A local business gets found one city at a time. We built a dedicated City Page for each community the practice serves (Encino, Tarzana, Sherman Oaks, Studio City, Van Nuys, and neighbors), and each one has genuinely different writing: its own directions to the office, its own local details. Not a template with the town name swapped in.

That difference matters, because Google punishes what it calls doorway pages, cookie-cutter pages that are identical except for the place name. We even cut some cities that were a stretch, to keep the quality high. Winnable local searches, one honest page each. That’s what put the practice on page one. (It’s also how you fix an AI that’s describing your locations wrong.)

Play 6: Claim your Google Business Profile

The map (that little pack of pins that sits above the regular blue links on a local search) is a completely separate race, and it’s won almost entirely by your Google Business Profile and its reviews. It’s the single most powerful local tool there is: for a search like “GLP-1 doctor near me,” the map is the very first thing a patient sees.

So we claimed and fully filled out the profile: correct name and address, the right categories, a real description, services, and photos. Getting onto the map is the profile itself; climbing up the map is reviews, which is the slow, ongoing part. Set the profile up first: it’s the foundation everything local rests on.

Play 7: Make sure Google can find the site, fast

None of this works if Google never looks at your pages. A few housekeeping basics are your developer’s job: a sitemap (simply a list of all your pages that you hand to Google), pages that load quickly on a phone, and clean web addresses.

Then comes the step almost everyone skips. Go into Google Search Console (Google’s free dashboard for site owners) and, for each page, click “Request indexing.” That’s you raising your hand and saying, come look at this now, instead of waiting days or weeks for Google to wander over on its own. It’s free, it takes minutes, and it’s the difference between showing up this week and next month.

What “page one in a week” does and doesn’t mean

It means page one for specific, high-intent, lower-competition searches, the ones actually worth winning for a local business. It does not mean we outrank hospital systems for “childhood obesity,” that we own the map pack yet (reviews take time to build up), or that a search for a doctor’s own name instantly beats his decades-old hospital profile. Those are a longer game.

Fast wins come from picking fights you can win. Anyone promising you page one for a big, competitive keyword in a week is selling something.

Steal this checklist

  • Chase the specific searches, not the obvious ones. Go after the exact, local phrases your customers actually type before the big, broad keywords everyone fights over.
  • Ask your web developer to add “structured data” (schema markup) to every page, so Google and AI tools understand your business, your people, and your services.
  • Start each important page by answering a real customer question in plain language, before any sales pitch.
  • Show your real credentials, and link your site to your established profiles (hospital pages, directories, LinkedIn) so Google connects them to you. (Your developer will know these as “same as” links.)
  • Make a separate, genuinely different page for each city or area you serve, never copies with the town name swapped in.
  • Claim your Google Business Profile, fill it out completely, and start asking happy customers for reviews: that’s how you show up on the map.
  • Give Google a sitemap and use Search Console to “request indexing” so your pages show up in days, not weeks.
