Wineries’ Biggest Fear Is Selling. So Why Is All Their AI Pointed at the Cellar?

InnoVint just surveyed 541 wineries. Their data says the problem is demand — and it opens a natural next question about where AI could help most.

Every year InnoVint publishes its State of Winery Health Report, and the 2026 edition — 541 winery professionals — opens with a number that should stop every owner cold.

Eighty percent named maintaining or growing sales as a top concern. Forty-four percent called it their single biggest worry — more than four times any other fear on the list. Everything else that keeps winemakers up at night ladders directly back to it: tasting-room traffic (45%), losing customers to other brands and beverages (32%), and — most tellingly — brand recognition in the market (25%).

The report doesn’t hedge about what this means. In its words: “If demand can be addressed, many of these issues would likely ease as well.”

So the industry’s own flagship survey is clear: the hardest problem in wine right now isn’t making it. It’s getting someone to find it and buy it.

Here’s the part that should change what you do Monday

For the first time, the survey asked wineries where they use AI. Sixty percent already use it somewhere. But look at where:

  • Sales & marketing — 43%
  • Tasting room — 20%
  • Winemaking — 19%
  • Finance — 11%
  • Compliance — 8%
  • Inventory — 6%

Every one of those is AI pointed inward — wineries putting AI to work inside their own operations, from the cellar to the tasting room to the books.

The survey captures where wineries put AI to work themselves. A natural companion question sits right alongside it: not where you use AI, but whether the AI your buyer now uses to shop can find and recommend you.

That’s the opening. Most winery AI today goes toward the work you do yourself — and there’s a quieter, higher-leverage job sitting right next to it: becoming discoverable to the assistant a buyer opens when they type “a crisp white for oysters.” They’re two different jobs. One helps you run the winery. The other helps a new buyer find it.

Why this is the opportunity, not the bad news

The same report offers the tell. It found that AI only pays off for wineries that use it with discipline — “AI helps wineries that are already operating well; it will not fix things for those that aren’t.” The wineries seeing real returns aren’t the ones with the flashiest tool. They’re the ones who pointed it at the right problem.

Right now the right problem — the one 44% of the industry named their single biggest worry — is one of the least-touched by AI. Whoever turns their attention outward first tends to get found while everyone else is still using AI to email the people who already follow them.

What “pointing it outward” actually means

It isn’t a new marketing budget. It’s making sure the machine can read you:

  1. Put your facts in plain text — grape, region, style, price, pairings — not locked in a PDF or baked into an image a machine can’t read.
  2. Add structured data so an assistant can label your product, price, and reviews without guessing. (Here’s how structured data works, in plain English.)
  3. Write the pages buyers actually ask for — occasion, budget, and pairing questions, answered directly.
  4. Let the AI crawlers in, so the assistants can see you at all.

None of it takes a bigger operation or a prime location — the same thing InnoVint says about every other healthy-winery habit in the report.

Most of the industry’s new AI energy is going into making the wine itself better — and that’s genuinely exciting. The report names the demand challenge plainly, and the opportunity it opens is to point some of that same energy outward too: toward the AI that’s increasingly how buyers decide what to pour.
